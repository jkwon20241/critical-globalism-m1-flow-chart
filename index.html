Lisa LoweThe Intimacies of Four ContinentsDuke University Press, 2015 · Chapters 1, 3, and 5

The liberal human

Chapters 1, 3, and 5 of The Intimacies of Four Continents treat “the human” as a product of liberal philosophy, the colonial archive, and the trade in goods and people that connected Europe, Africa, Asia, and the Americas.

Chapter 1

Liberal universalism

“the modern distinction between definitions of the human and those to whom such definitions do not extend is the condition of possibility for Western liberalism, and not its particular exception” (Lowe 3)

Lowe opens by reversing the familiar story in which liberalism gradually extends freedom to everyone. A definition of the human is already an act of sorting, since to define is to draw an edge. The phrase condition of possibility means that the excluded are the ground on which the liberal subject stands. Thus slavery, settlement, and indenture are not a particular exception, a lapse that progress will eventually correct; they are the structure that lets liberal freedom be imagined at all.

Chapter 1

Colonial divisions of humanity

“as modern liberalism defined the ‘human’ and universalized its attributes to European man, it simultaneously differentiated populations in the colonies as less than human” (Lowe 6)

The key word is simultaneously: universalizing and differentiating are one gesture, not two. To universalize is to take the attributes of one figure, European man, and present them as humanity’s own. The colonial divisions of humanity are the resulting distances from that figure, which Lowe lists as “Indian,” “Black,” “Negro,” “Chinese,” and “coolie” (8). Moreover, she calls race “an enduring remainder” of this process (7). A remainder is what is left over once the human has been “freed,” a residue that keeps returning to unsettle liberalism’s claim to be finished.

Chapters 1 and 3

Colonial division of intimacy

“a colonial division of intimacy, which charts the historically differentiated access to the domains of liberal personhood, from interiority and individual will, to the possession of property and domesticity” (Lowe 18)

Lowe uses intimacy as a heuristic with two senses. In its dominant sense it is the private life of what C. B. Macpherson called “the possessive individual” (18): marriage, family, the bourgeois home. In an older, now rare sense it means “close connexion” (19), which Lowe extends to the alliances among enslaved, Indigenous, and colonized peoples across continents. Differentiated access shows that interiority (an inner life), individual will, property, and domesticity (the household) are rationed goods. Consequently, Man is the one permitted an inside and a home, while the laborers who furnish that home are kept outside it.

Chapters 1, 3, and 5

The economy of affirmation and forgetting

“the economy of affirmation and forgetting that structures and formalizes the archives of liberalism, and liberal ways of understanding. This economy civilizes and develops freedoms for ‘man’ in modern Europe and North America, while relegating others to geographical and temporal spaces that are constituted as backward, uncivilized, and unfree” (Lowe 3)

Economy implies an exchange: every affirmation of liberal progress is paid for with a forgetting. The archive is not neutral storage; Lowe describes the British National Archives as divided by office and region, with separate records for settlement, the slave trade, abolition, and Chinese emigration, so that their connections are hard to read (5). The paired verbs civilizes and relegating show the two directions of this economy: it raises some people into “civilization” and pushes others down and away. The word temporal matters as well, because the “unfree” are placed in the past, behind Europe. In Chapter 5 Lowe answers with a past conditional temporality, a way of reading for “what could have been” that treats the past as “a configuration of multiple contingent possibilities, all present, yet none inevitable” (175).

Sylvia Wynter“Unsettling the Coloniality of Being/Power/Truth/Freedom: Towards the Human, After Man, Its Overrepresentation—An Argument”CR: The New Centennial Review, 2003

Man and its Others

Where Lowe describes the division, Wynter names its engine. She argues that Europe rewrote its idea of the human twice: “as Christian becomes Man1 (as political subject), then as Man1 becomes Man2 (as a bio-economic subject)” (318), and each time a new population was made to stand for the Other.

pp. 260–62

The overrepresentation of Man

“our present ethnoclass (i.e., Western bourgeois) conception of the human, Man, which overrepresents itself as if it were the human itself” (Wynter 260)

Ethnoclass fuses ethnicity and class: Man is the self-image of one specific Western bourgeois group. To overrepresent is to fill the whole frame with that one genre of being human, and as if marks the fiction. Wynter calls such a self-image a descriptive statement (262), a culture’s working answer to the question of who and what we are. Therefore, she frames the central struggle of our time as the “Man vs. Human struggle” (261), which links Lowe’s “particular” liberal subject to a claim about the whole species.

pp. 263–66

Man1: the political subject and the Human Other

the peoples of the Americas and of Black Africa “were made to reoccupy the matrix slot of Otherness—to be made into the physical referent of the idea of the irrational/subrational Human Other” (Wynter 266)

In Man1, Renaissance humanism and the rising state replace the “True Christian Self” with Man as “the political subject of the state” (263). The matrix slot is a structural position older than its occupants: in medieval Christendom it held heretics and infidels, and from the sixteenth century onward it is filled by “Indians” and “Negroes” (265–66). A physical referent is a body drafted to make an abstract idea visible. The pairing irrational/subrational marks the new dividing line: where the Christian Other lacked true faith, Man1’s Other is said to lack reason, or to have less of it. Thus Wynter supplies the mechanism behind Lowe’s “less than human”: the slot exists first, and colonial violence fills it.

pp. 318–21

Man2: the economic human

“Man2 is now defined as a jobholding Breadwinner, and even more optimally, as a successful ‘masterer of Natural Scarcity’ (Investor, or capital accumulator)” (Wynter 321)

After Malthus and Darwin, Man takes what Wynter calls a “purely biocentric and homo oeconomicus form” (282). Natural Scarcity replaces Original Sin as the “significant ill,” so salvation becomes material rather than spiritual (320). The Breadwinner is the minimum standard of full humanity, and the Investor is its ideal. Consequently, whoever fails economically is read as having failed to be fully human, which is the point where Wynter meets racial capitalism.

pp. 321–25

The archipelago of Human Otherness

“the new descriptive statement of the human will call for its archipelago of Human Otherness to be peopled by a new category, one now comprised of the jobless, the homeless, the Poor, the systemically made jobless and criminalized” (Wynter 321)

An archipelago is a chain of islands: scattered and separated, yet one system. The phrase systemically made insists that joblessness is produced, not natural. Wynter adds the psychic cost of this order: the “dysselected” become the mode of non-being “that each individual and group must strive to avoid, struggle to prove that they themselves are not, if they are to be” (325). In the end, this is the idea that Parasite dramatizes.

Koshy, Cacho, Byrd, and Jefferson, editorsColonial Racial CapitalismDuke University Press, 2022 · Introduction and Chapters 2, 3, and 8

The human as value

The introduction, Goldstein (Chapter 2), Harris (Chapter 3), and Day (Chapter 8) show how the division between Man and its Others is carried out through land, family, debt, and technology.

Introduction

Racial capitalism

“The tendency of European civilization through capitalism was thus not to homogenize but to differentiate—to exaggerate regional, subcultural, and dialectical differences into ‘racial’ ones.” (Robinson, qtd. in Koshy et al. 2)

Racial capitalism, as the editors take it from Cedric Robinson, argues that racism is not added onto capitalism. Capitalism does not homogenize, or make everyone the same; it works through differentiation, turning ordinary local differences into ranked “racial” ones. Following Robinson, they explain that racial logics name the differences behind inequality as innate, “as ‘biological,’ ‘cultural,’ ‘environmental,’ and so forth” (2). Drawing on Ruth Wilson Gilmore, they add that “racial does not necessarily mean Black or require white” (2). Furthermore, they cite Chandan Reddy, whose description of life that “exists without value or meaning for westernized-man” (6) joins this branch to Wynter’s.

Introduction, pp. 5–7, and Goldstein, Chapter 2

Recursive dispossession and social reproduction

“Racial capitalism is colonial capitalism, especially where settler and imperial thefts of land, the production of hierarchies of global space, and the expropriation of labor occur by means of recursive processes that require possession and rights in order to produce dispossession and rightlessness.” (Koshy et al. 7)

Recursive processes loop back on themselves: following Rob Nichols, the editors explain that land is first turned into property so that it can be taken, and that dispossession “produces what it presupposes” (5). Possession and rights, the goods of liberal Man, become the very instruments of rightlessness. Hierarchies of global space rank whole regions as developed or “backward,” which, the editors note, lets dispossession be renamed “a problem of development” (3). Goldstein carries this into family life: the notion of filius nullius (“nobody’s child”) in the removal of Native children works alongside terra nullius in land policy (16), and inheritance law has broken up Native and Black landholding. For him, social reproduction is “a dialectical relation that distributes attributions of disposability and premature death” (76–77).

Cheryl I. Harris, Chapter 3

The racial alchemy of debt

“debt functions as a form of racial alchemy: it obscures highly racialized processes and racially differentiated burdens, and it normalizes and makes available for generalized application ever-more-rapacious and predatory forms of extraction” (Harris 95)

Alchemy is the false science of turning one substance into another; here a formally race-neutral instrument turns racial subordination into an asset. Although abolition ended the value of Black people as property, Harris writes, “the racial alchemy of debt transformed freed people into assets yet again” (93). Predatory inclusion names “an illusory project of racial liberalism” that offers inclusion in the market as the cure for Black subordination (96). Extraction means drawing value out of people without return, and Harris warns that once these methods are normalized they become available for “generalized application,” spreading beyond the groups they first targeted. Thus Wynter’s Investor and his debtor are produced together, as two sides of one ledger.

Iyko Day, Chapter 8

Colonial unknowing

“the antipolitical frame of technopolitics reveals the coordinated expansion and technological intensification of imperial state power that is secured through its simultaneous depoliticization” (Day 258)

Day begins with Obama’s 2016 Hiroshima speech, which turned the bomb into a lesson about “humanity’s core contradiction” (257), a universal humanity once more. Technopolitics is politics carried out through technology; Day argues that after decolonization, relations between colonizer and colonized were remade as relations between the nuclear and the non-nuclear (258). Antipolitics and depoliticization describe how that power is framed as neutral technology, nobody’s decision. The result is what Day, citing Vimalassery, Pegues, and Goldstein, calls colonial unknowing (258): the uranium that came from the Belgian Congo and Canada’s Northwest Territories disappears behind the mushroom cloud, and Indigenous lands become nonsites of nuclear modernity (258–59). This is Lowe’s economy of forgetting in a nuclear register.

Three examples

Two examples come from the readings; the third, Parasite, carries the concept beyond the texts.

Example 1 · Lisa Lowe, Chapter 3

Silk stands for opium

Joshua Bates “identified himself by saying, ‘I am a silk trader,’ which meant, ‘I trade in opium’” (Lowe 97)

In testimony to the Parliamentary Select Committee on the China trade, silk was the legitimate cargo that screened the illicit opium trade: opium sold in Canton paid for the silk shipped home (96–97). Lowe sets this beside Thackeray’s Vanity Fair, where Becky Sharp’s pink dress “looked as fresh as a rose” (96). Metaphor carries meaning from one thing to another, and Lowe reads it as a model of commodity exchange, a process that “both evokes through equivalence and occludes through exchange” (97). Consequently, the English drawing room, the dominant form of intimacy, is furnished by India’s opium farmers, enslaved cotton pickers in the American South, and the “coolie” trade that grew after the Opium Wars (97–98). This example demonstrates the full concept: Man’s private life is built from labor the archive forgets, and the consumer at its center is already Wynter’s economic Man.

Example 2 · Cheryl I. Harris, Chapter 3, with Wynter

Cameron Rowland’s Leveler Rings

“Manhole leveler rings are cast by prisoners in Elmira Correctional Facility. […] The road is a public asset, instrumental to commercial development.” (Rowland, qtd. in Harris 89–90)

Harris opens with a work from Rowland’s 2016 exhibition 91020000: large stacked aluminum rings, “stubbornly ordinary” (89), cast by prisoners paid between $0.10 and $1.14 an hour (90). The rings keep the road smooth, a public asset held for everyone yet built by people with almost no rights, and the object presents itself as raceless. Yet Harris insists that “the racelessness of the object is illusory” (90), because its genealogy “begins with enslavement and extends through its many afterlives” (90), including convict leasing and the chain gang. Like Bates’s silk, the ring hides the labor inside it. Moreover, the example joins Wynter and Harris: the “criminalized” members of Wynter’s archipelago are put to work, and debt and incarceration are the infrastructure that keeps them there. The Nas lyric among Wynter’s epigraphs, about taxpayers paying for more jails for Black and Latino people (258), states the same relation from the street.

Example 3 · Bong Joon Ho, Parasite (2019)

The line, the smell, the flood

Scene A · Under the coffee table

Hidden beneath the Parks’ living-room table, the Kim family listens as Mr. Park tells his wife that their driver, Ki-taek, never crosses the line, but that his smell “crosses the line.” He compares it to an old radish and to the smell of people on the subway. The line is Wynter’s line between Man and its Human Other, and the smell, which the semi-basement leaves in Ki-taek’s clothes, is exactly the kind of “biological” difference the editors describe: a class difference treated as a fact of the body. Moreover, the staging literalizes Lowe’s colonial division of intimacy. The Kims are physically closer to the Parks than anyone, yet they lie beneath the household’s private life, and the couple’s intimate moment rests on top of them. Meanwhile, the Kims’ whole scheme of forged documents and invented credentials is Wynter’s “struggle to prove that they themselves are not.”

Scene B · The flood and the party

That night the rain floods the Kims’ semi-basement with sewage, and the family sleeps in a gymnasium among other evacuees. The next morning Mrs. Park cheerfully mentions the clear, dust-free sky the rain has left and prepares a garden party. One storm produces two worlds: Wynter’s Natural Scarcity falls along class lines, and the Parks’ ignorance of the flood resembles Day’s colonial unknowing on the scale of a single city. Beneath the house, Geun-sae, who has hidden in the bunker for years from loan sharks, taps out “Respect!” to Mr. Park with the lights; he is Harris’s debtor, underground and invisible, honoring Wynter’s “masterer of Natural Scarcity.” Finally, Ki-woo’s plan to earn enough money to buy the house plays out as a fantasy before the film returns him to the semi-basement. In Lowe’s terms, it is a past conditional that liberal mobility cannot fulfill.

Conclusion: toward the human, after Man

Lowe, Wynter, and Day

Other humanities

“We are left with the project of imagining, mourning, and reckoning ‘other humanities’ within the received genealogy of ‘the human.’” (Lowe 175)

Each text ends by refusing to complete Man’s story. The received genealogy of “the human” is the inherited liberal history Lowe has traced, and to reckon with it is to account for what it cost. Lowe’s other humanities are plural, and they are to be imagined and mourned rather than recovered whole. Wynter’s title points “Towards the Human, After Man”: a species no longer defined by one ethnoclass. Day finds a model in Takahiro Iwasaki’s dioramas made of hair, towels, and toothbrushes, whose failure to be useful “represents a queer human temporality that resists absorption into the abstract temporality of the commodity form” (278). In the end, the concept of Man / the Human develops across these readings from a philosophical definition (Lowe) into a mechanism of Otherness (Wynter) and finally into a system of value, debt, and forgetting (Colonial Racial Capitalism). Parasite shows that the same mechanism now organizes life far from its European origin.
